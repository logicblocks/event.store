# What hybrid push-and-poll patterns reconcile notification efficiency with polling durability — notify as a wake-up hint over a durable log, safety-net polling, adaptive and backoff polling, long-polling, and coalescing or debouncing of notification bursts — and how are missed notifications detected and recovered from?

## Question

Which designs combine push notification (for low latency and low idle cost) with polling (for durability and failure tolerance)? The patterns in scope are: treating a notification as a wake-up hint over a durable log, safety-net polling, adaptive and backoff polling, long-polling, and coalescing or debouncing bursts of notifications. The question also asks how a consumer detects that it has missed notifications and how it recovers.

## Findings

No scholarly work examined here studies database LISTEN and NOTIFY consumers directly. Four nearby bodies of work all reach the same design, though:

- push-pull dissemination of changing data,
- hybrids of interrupts and polling in operating-system network stacks,
- reliable multicast in which the receiver is responsible for reliability,
- lease-based cache consistency.

Web long-polling work adds the transport view. The shared conclusion is that **push is a latency optimisation and pull is the correctness mechanism**. The notification never carries data of record. It only tells a consumer that reading the durable source is worthwhile now. A periodic or timeout-driven pull puts an upper limit on how long any failure of the push path can go unnoticed.

### Push and pull have complementary failure properties

Bhide et al. frame the trade-off in its most general form. They compare push and pull for keeping clients temporally coherent with fast-changing data, and find the two complementary across four properties:

- the coherency they achieve,
- communication overhead,
- the state the server must hold,
- coherency lost when the server fails.

Push keeps coherency high and sends messages only when something changes. In exchange, the server must hold state for every client, and a failure or broken connection silently stops updates. Pull needs no server state and survives failures. Its interval, though, is either too short (wasted requests) or too long (stale data).

Their answer is a combined, adaptive scheme that fits dissemination to server and proxy load and to each client's coherency needs. They name resilience to failure as an explicit goal alongside scalability.

- **Push-and-Pull:** the client keeps pulling on an adaptively computed time-to-refresh, while the server pushes the changes those pulls would miss. If the push path fails, the pull schedule carries on, so coherency degrades gracefully rather than collapsing.
- **Push-or-Pull:** the server decides per client whether to push or to leave the client to pull, depending on available resources.

For an event store, the mapping is direct. The append-only log is the authoritative server state. The notification is the push path, and it is allowed to fail. The consumer's read from its checkpoint is the pull path, and it alone guarantees that nothing is lost.

### Notify as a wake-up hint: interrupt-then-poll

Mogul and Ramakrishnan's work on receive livelock is the clearest precedent for using a notification purely as a trigger for polling. Systems driven entirely by interrupts have low overhead and good latency at light load. At high arrival rates they degrade into livelock: all their time goes on handling interrupts, and at the extreme no work reaches the application. Their fix was to schedule network processing as carefully as process execution:

1. An interrupt only schedules a polling routine, and further interrupts are suppressed.
2. The poller processes pending work to completion in bounded quotas.
3. Interrupts are re-enabled only once the backlog is drained.

The result is interrupts at light load and polling at heavy load.

Applied to a log consumer, this gives a precise contract:

- **The notification handler does nothing except mark the consumer runnable.** It does not parse or trust the payload, and does no work itself. Duplicate, out-of-order, merged or missing notifications therefore cannot cause a correctness error. The worst a missing one can do is delay a read.
- **On waking, the consumer drains the log from its checkpoint in bounded batches until a read comes back short or empty, and only then waits again.** The batch size is the quota. It bounds memory and latency per iteration and keeps the consumer responsive to shutdown and to other work.
- **Under sustained load the consumer stays in drain mode and notifications become redundant.** This is the livelock lesson: when every read finds work, reacting to each notification only adds overhead. Continuous batched polling is the efficient regime, and notifications matter only for the transition out of idle.

### Coalescing and debouncing bursts

The drain loop coalesces bursts by construction. Any number of notifications that arrive while a drain is running reduce to "check once more". The wake signal should therefore be *level-triggered*: a single pending flag or single-slot event, not a queue of per-event callbacks. The one correctness rule is ordering. **Clear the pending flag before starting the drain, never after it.** If the flag is cleared after the drain, a notification that arrived mid-drain is erased while its event may still be unread. That is a lost wakeup, and only the safety net would recover it.

Debouncing goes a step further and deliberately waits a short window after the first wake-up before draining. It trades a little latency for larger batches. It is worth doing when writes arrive in bursts and per-read overhead dominates. In the interrupt literature's terms, it moves the crossover from interrupt-style to polling-style behaviour to a lower load.

### Safety-net polling as a lease on silence

Notifications are lost in ordinary operation, not only in disasters:

- a listener's connection drops or is recycled,
- a consumer process restarts,
- the notifying path fails between the commit and delivery.

Gray and Cheriton's leases give the right model for bounding the damage. A lease grants a cache holder rights for a limited term. A holder that cannot be reached, or a lost message, therefore affects consistency for at most that term, after which validity must be re-established. They argue that comparatively short terms capture most of the benefit at modest overhead.

Read as a lease, the consumer's belief that nothing new has arrived since its last read is valid only for a term T. When T expires without a wake-up, the consumer polls anyway. Any single lost notification then costs at most T of extra latency, never lost events.

This changes how the interval is chosen compared with pure polling. A pure-polling interval is set by the latency you want. A safety-net interval is set by the staleness you can tolerate under failure. Because notifications handle the common case, T can be much longer than the fixed polling interval it replaces, which cuts idle query load substantially.

### Adaptive and backoff polling

Bhide et al.'s pull side adapts its time-to-refresh to how fast the data is changing relative to the client's tolerance. It shortens when changes come close to that tolerance, lengthens when the data is quiet, and stays within configured minimum and maximum bounds. In a hybrid consumer, the same adaptation fits the safety-net interval, driven by evidence about the push path rather than about the data:

- **Back off towards T-max** while timeout-driven polls keep finding nothing. That is evidence the notifications are healthy and nothing was missed.
- **Shrink towards T-min** when a timeout-driven poll finds events, because that means a notification was missed. Shrink too after reconnecting the notification channel, and whenever the channel looks unhealthy.
- **Stop waiting and keep polling** while drains keep returning full batches. This is the heavy-load regime from the livelock work, and Push-or-Pull's server-side decision has the same shape: fall back to pull when the push path is overloaded.

With many consumers, randomising (jittering) safety-net timers prevents synchronised bursts of queries. Scalable reliable multicast uses randomised timers in the same way, to keep many receivers from requesting repairs at once.

### Long-polling: a wait with a timeout is the hybrid

Long-polling simulates server push by holding a request open until an event arrives or a timeout fires. Shuang and Kai compare plain polling, long polling, streaming and WebSocket on delivery delay and on unnecessary connections. These are exactly the axes on which plain polling loses: its latency is bounded by the interval, and its cost is spent on empty requests. Stratmann, Ousterhout and Madan's Vault shows that the hard part of long-polling is not the mechanism but integrating it with application frameworks and scaling it across clusters of hundreds of application servers. For a library, this argues for hiding the wait-and-recheck loop behind the consumer abstraction rather than exposing notification plumbing to users.

Structurally, a consumer that blocks waiting for a notification with a timeout *is* a long poll, and the timeout *is* the safety-net lease. One loop therefore covers both the wake-up hint and the safety net:

```text
subscribe to notifications            # arm before the first read
loop:
    clear pending flag
    drain from checkpoint in bounded batches until a short read
    wait for a notification or timeout T
    adapt T from whether the wake was a notification or a timeout,
        and whether the drain found events
on channel reconnect: drain unconditionally and shrink T
```

The same shape appears outside the web. Zhai describes replacing short-interval database polling, which loads the database, with a push service built from database triggers and a message queue. Apple's DNS Long-Lived Queries extended a query protocol with change notification, so clients learn of changes without polling. That protocol was later superseded by a standards-track push-notification design.

### Detecting and recovering missed notifications

Scalable reliable multicast (Floyd et al.) gives the most developed model of loss detection. Its key points:

- **Receivers own reliability.** Each receiver, not the sender, tracks what it has and asks for what it lacks.
- **Data is named by source and sequence number**, so a gap in received sequence numbers reveals a loss.
- **Tail loss is detected by periodic session messages.** When the last packet is lost, no later packet exposes the gap, so each member periodically advertises the highest sequence number it has seen.
- **Repair requests use randomised timers** to suppress duplicates.

Carried over to a consumer on a durable log:

1. **Recovery is always a re-read from the consumer's own checkpoint.** The log is the retransmission buffer, so no server-side state about consumers is needed. Because the consumer drains from its checkpoint on every wake, gaps in notifications heal themselves. Explicit gap detection is then mainly for monitoring and for tuning T, not for correctness.
2. **Gap detection needs notifications that carry a position.** If a notification includes the log position of the newly appended event, the consumer can compare it with its checkpoint and with the previous notification it saw. A jump shows that notifications were missed or merged.
3. **Tail loss needs a session-message equivalent.** A cheap periodic probe of the log head's position, compared with the checkpoint, detects the case where the *last* notification was lost and nothing has followed. The safety-net poll is this probe, either as a full read or as a lighter head-position check.
4. **Treat every reconnection of the notification channel as a known window of loss.** Notifications sent while the listener was disconnected were never delivered, so the consumer should drain unconditionally the moment it re-subscribes.
5. **Count timeout-triggered drains that find events.** That count directly measures missed notifications in production, and it is the right input for adaptive safety-net intervals.

One caution applies to gap detection on a database-backed log. It assumes positions are dense and become visible in order. If positions are allocated before commit, a gap may be temporary (a transaction still in flight) or permanent (a rollback). Gap-based detection must therefore tolerate gaps rather than treat each one as a lost notification. Otherwise it will either stall waiting for events that will never exist, or move past events that are about to become visible.

### Synthesis

The robust hybrid looks like this:

- an authoritative, ordered durable log;
- a consumer that tracks its own position and always reads from it;
- a level-triggered, coalescing wake-up signal that carries no data of record;
- a bounded wait (a long poll) whose timeout is a lease on silence and adapts to evidence about the notification path;
- unconditional catch-up on start-up and on every reconnect;
- a switch to continuous batched draining under load.

In this design, missed notifications cost latency of at most the current safety-net interval and never correctness. They are detected as timeout-driven or reconnect-driven drains that find work, and as head-position probes running ahead of the checkpoint.
