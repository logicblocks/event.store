# How can a consumer detect silent failure of its LISTEN connection, for example through periodic heartbeat notifications, and how should the heartbeat period and timeout be set from measured notification delay and loss?

## Question

A consumer waiting on a PostgreSQL LISTEN connection gets no signal at all if
that connection dies silently, for example through a half-open TCP socket, a
middlebox dropping an idle flow, or a server failover. Silence then looks the
same as "no new events". How can the consumer detect this, for instance with
periodic heartbeat notifications? And how should the heartbeat period and the
suspicion timeout be derived from the notification delay and loss the
consumer actually measures?

## Findings

The sources here are general distributed-systems work on failure detectors,
not PostgreSQL material. Wherever this note applies them to LISTEN, NOTIFY or
psycopg, it marks that step as inference.

**Silence can only ever raise suspicion, so acting on a wrong suspicion must
be safe.** Chandra and Toueg's study of unreliable failure detectors
describes detectors by two properties, completeness (real failures are
eventually suspected) and accuracy (how far correct processes avoid being
suspected). It also shows that useful detectors can still make mistakes, even
infinitely many. A timeout over an asynchronous channel cannot tell a dead
connection from a slow one, so a heartbeat timeout gives a suspicion, not
proof. Heartbeat counters alone don't help either. Aguilera, Chen and Toueg's
timeout-free "heartbeat" detector only produces growing counters, which
serve quiescent reliable communication. Later work on that model notes that
an algorithm using it "can do nothing without time-out technology" when it
has to separate crashed nodes from correct ones. A consumer needs a verdict,
so it needs a timeout. (Inference:) in an event store, the natural response
to a suspicion is to close the connection, reconnect, re-issue LISTEN and
then read the log from the consumer's checkpoint. That response is
idempotent. A false suspicion costs one reconnect and one catch-up query, and
never correctness. Consensus systems pay much more for a wrong suspicion, so
this asymmetry justifies tuning the consumer for faster detection than they
would use.

**The mechanism.** Something emits a NOTIFY at a fixed period η, either on
the consumer's channel or on a heartbeat channel the consumer also listens
on. Each heartbeat carries a sequence number and a send timestamp. The
consumer records when each one arrives and suspects the connection when no
fresh heartbeat has arrived by a deadline. Satzger et al.'s "lazy
monitoring" shows that ordinary application messages can stand in for
heartbeats, which cuts overhead and gives the detector more information about
current network conditions. (Inference:) a real event notification is
therefore also proof that the connection is alive, and explicit heartbeats
are only strictly needed during idle periods. The trade-off is that event
notifications arrive irregularly and carry no heartbeat sequence number, so
mixing them in pushes the detector towards the simple "reset the timer on
every receipt" rule discussed below.

**What to measure and what to require.** Chen, Toueg and Aguilera define the
quality of service (QoS) of a failure detector for systems where message
delay D and loss probability p_L follow probability distributions, which are
exactly the "measured delay and loss" a consumer can collect. The main
metrics are:

- **Detection time T_D**: from a real failure until permanent suspicion.
- **Mistake recurrence time T_MR**: the time between false suspicions.
- **Mistake duration T_M**: how long a false suspicion lasts.

They also define derived measures such as the average mistake rate and the
query accuracy probability.

(Inference:) for a consumer, these map as follows:

- An upper bound on T_D is how long the consumer may stay deaf before it
  notices. It should be clearly shorter than the safety-net poll interval,
  otherwise the poll already bounds staleness and the heartbeat adds little.
- A lower bound on T_MR is the spurious-reconnect rate you can tolerate. For
  N consumers the fleet-wide rate is about N / T_MR.
- T_M matters less, because a suspicion ends in a reconnect rather than a
  period of wrong output.

Measuring the inputs: delay comes from receive time minus send timestamp
when sender and receiver share a clock. Otherwise it comes from the jitter
in inter-arrival times. Loss shows up as gaps in the heartbeat sequence on a
connection that otherwise stayed up.

**Setting the period and timeout: the Chen–Toueg–Aguilera rule.** In their
detector (NFD-S), the sender emits heartbeat i at time σ_i = iη. The
receiver fixes "freshness points" τ_i = σ_i + δ and trusts the sender at τ_i
only if some heartbeat numbered i or higher has arrived by then. This ties
the deadline to the send schedule rather than to the arrival of the last
message, and has these consequences:

- **Worst-case detection.** T_D ≤ η + δ.
- **Chance of a false suspicion.** A false suspicion at a freshness point
  needs every heartbeat that could have arrived by then to be lost or late.
  In the paper's analysis its probability is
  p_0 = ∏_{j=0}^{⌈δ/η⌉−1} [p_L + (1 − p_L)·Pr(D > δ − jη)].
- **Mean time between false suspicions.** This is about η / p_0.
- **Configuring η and δ.** Given an upper bound on T_D, a lower bound on
  T_MR and the measured loss and delay distribution, the paper's procedure
  sets δ = T_D^U − η. It then looks for the largest η that still meets the
  accuracy requirements, because a larger η means fewer heartbeats. If no η
  qualifies, the requirement cannot be met on that network.
- **Partial measurements.** The authors show the procedure still works with
  only estimates of p_L, E(D) and V(D), by using a one-sided Chebyshev bound
  in place of the true delay tail.
- **Comparison with the common rule.** In their simulations this detector
  beats the commonly used rule of restarting a fixed timer on every received
  heartbeat.

**The main trade-off.** For a fixed detection bound η + δ, a shorter period
puts more heartbeats inside each window. All of them must fail for a false
suspicion to happen, so the false-suspicion rate falls roughly geometrically
in δ/η, while the cost in messages grows only linearly.

(Inference, worked example:) say the target detection bound is 10 s, loss on
a healthy connection is negligible, and the measured delay tail beyond 5 s is
about 10⁻⁶. Then η = δ = 5 s gives p_0 ≈ 10⁻⁶ and a mean time between
spurious reconnects of about 5 s / 10⁻⁶, roughly two months per consumer. If
loss is significant, say p_L = 10⁻², the same choice gives only about 500 s,
or roughly 8 minutes. Shortening η to 2 s with δ = 8 s puts four heartbeats
in each window and brings p_0 down to about 10⁻⁸.

**When clocks differ or delay drifts: estimating expected arrivals plus an
adaptive margin.** If the heartbeat comes from a different host, for example
a database-side scheduler or another process, sender and receiver clocks are
not synchronised. Chen et al. then estimate each heartbeat's expected arrival
time EA from the receiver's own record of recent arrivals. The heartbeat
period is known, so each recent arrival, shifted by its period offset,
predicts the next one. The freshness point becomes EA + α, where α is a
safety margin. This uses only the receiver's clock.

Bertier, Marin and Sens split the problem in two. A basic estimate of the
expected arrival time keeps detection short, and a separate safety margin
adapts the QoS to what the application needs. That margin is computed in the
style of Jacobson's TCP retransmission timer: a smoothed estimate of the
delay plus a multiple of its smoothed mean deviation. Jacobson introduced
that estimator because timers set from the mean alone fire spuriously when
delay variance rises under load. Yang et al. instead use exponential
smoothing to predict the next arrival, which also follows linear trends.
When that prediction misses, they run a second "interaction" step that
probes the peer actively before declaring it failed. Zhao et al. forecast
heartbeat delay with a small neural network trained on a sliding window of
past delays. That shows richer predictors are possible, though (inference) a
consumer gains little from them beyond what a smoothed mean and deviation
already give.

**Graded rather than binary suspicion.** Hayashibara et al.'s φ accrual
detector outputs a continuous suspicion level instead of a yes or no. It
fits a distribution to a sliding window of heartbeat inter-arrival times and
reports φ = −log10 of the probability that a heartbeat would arrive later
than the time already elapsed. A threshold Φ then sets the false-suspicion
likelihood at about 10^−Φ. (Inference:) this suits a consumer well, because
it can act in proportion to φ:

1. At a low φ, run a cheap catch-up read of the log, which hedges against a
   lost wake-up and costs little if the read finds nothing.
2. At a higher φ, actively probe the listening connection with a short
   deadline.
3. At the highest φ, tear the connection down and reconnect.

**Practical pitfalls the sources point to.**

1. **Stalls in the application.** Wang and Li note that heartbeats handled at
   the application layer are destabilised by scheduling and load changes.
   (Inference:) in a Python asyncio consumer, event-loop stalls and long
   synchronous work on the listening connection add to the delay. They
   should either be in the measured delay distribution, by timestamping at
   the point the consumer actually sees the notification, or be removed by
   giving LISTEN a dedicated connection.
2. **Correlated delay.** The QoS analysis assumes delays and losses are
   independent from one message to the next. (Inference:) a database-wide
   stall delays every consumer's heartbeat at once and can cause a
   synchronised reconnect storm. Probing before reconnecting, as in Yang et
   al.'s interaction step, and jittering reconnects both limit the damage.
3. **Bandwidth.** Wang and Kuo treat the total bandwidth spent on failure
   detection as a budget to constrain. (Inference:) one NOTIFY per period on
   a shared heartbeat channel reaches every listener on it at a cost
   independent of the number of consumers. Self-heartbeats from each of N
   consumers on one shared channel instead fan out to N² receipts per
   period.
4. **Who emits the heartbeat decides what it tests.** (Inference:) if a
   consumer emits its own heartbeat from a second connection, sender and
   receiver share a clock, so NFD-S and direct delay measurement apply. It
   also tests the whole delivery path from the server's notification queue
   to the consumer's socket. A heartbeat from an external emitter can stop
   for reasons that have nothing to do with the consumer. The consumer can
   tell which side failed only with an active probe on its own connection.

**Putting the recipe together (inference from the sources above):**

1. **Instrument.** Put a sequence number and a send timestamp on every
   heartbeat, and record the observed delay and the gaps in the sequence.
2. **Set targets.** Choose the detection bound from the staleness you accept
   during a listener failure, kept below the safety-net poll interval.
   Choose the false-suspicion bound from the fleet-wide reconnect rate you
   can tolerate.
3. **Set the period and timeout.** Set η and δ with the Chen–Toueg–Aguilera
   rule (η + δ equal to the detection bound, η as large as accuracy allows).
   Use smaller η, so several heartbeats fall in each window, when measured
   loss is significant.
4. **Adapt.** Adjust the margin online, with a Jacobson-style mean plus
   deviation or a φ threshold, so that the timeout follows changes in
   delay.
5. **Respond.** On suspicion, probe, then reconnect, re-issue LISTEN and
   catch up from the checkpoint.
