# Which published studies measure commit-time lock contention in PostgreSQL or other MVCC databases when every committing transaction must update one shared global resource, such as a queue, counter or sequence?

## Question

Which peer-reviewed or otherwise published studies measure the lock contention that arises at commit time when every committing transaction in PostgreSQL, or in another multi-version concurrency control (MVCC) database, must update the same shared global resource, such as a queue, a counter or a sequence?

## Findings

### The short answer: the measured evidence comes from research engines, not from PostgreSQL

No study in the scholarly catalogue isolates PostgreSQL itself and measures commit throughput when every transaction must touch one global queue, counter or sequence. The closest PostgreSQL-specific records are a reproducibility artefact for a study of isolation anomalies under contended workloads. It covers PostgreSQL, CockroachDB, MongoDB and a two-phase-locking middleware, and it measures the tail-latency cost of removing anomalies, not the cost of a single hot resource. There is also a methods paper on attributing database lock contention to application code paths (Knoche). Neither one quantifies a single global serialisation point.

The direct measurements come from a group of multicore concurrency-control studies built on research prototypes: DBx1000, Peloton, Shore-MT and Silo. Between them they cover the three ways a shared global resource can serialise commits:

1. a **global counter** that hands out timestamps or IDs;
2. a **hot tuple** whose lock is held until the transaction ends;
3. a **commit-time log flush** that extends how long those locks are held.

### Global counters: timestamp and transaction-ID allocation

Yu, Bezerra, Pavlo, Devadas and Stonebraker ("Staring into the Abyss", PVLDB 2014) evaluated seven concurrency-control schemes, including MVCC, on a simulated many-core machine scaling towards 1,000 cores. They found that no scheme scaled all the way. Each failed on a different bottleneck: lock thrashing, abort overhead, or **timestamp allocation from a shared counter**. Every timestamp-ordering scheme, MVCC included, needs a unique, monotonically increasing value per transaction. The authors compared ways of producing it:

- A mutex-protected counter performed worst.
- An atomic fetch-and-add counter scaled further, but still flattened as cores increased, because all cores contend on one cache line.
- Batched allocation and hardware-clock-based allocation pushed the ceiling higher.

The study is also the standard reference for how throughput collapses under skewed, high-contention workloads. In those workloads a small set of hot tuples, such as TPC-C's per-district next-order-ID counter when there are few warehouses, serialises every transaction that touches it.

Tu, Zheng, Kohler, Liskov and Madden (Silo, SOSP 2013) treat centralised transaction-ID assignment as a contention point to remove entirely. Their record states that Silo "avoids all centralized contention points, including that of centralized transaction ID assignment". Instead, Silo derives serial order from per-record IDs combined with coarse-grained epochs, and uses the epoch boundary for group commit and logging. Silo is the main design point showing that a serial order can exist without a per-transaction global counter. The trade-off is that order is only resolved at epoch granularity.

Wu, Arulraj, Lin, Xian and Pavlo ("An Empirical Evaluation of In-Memory Multi-Version Concurrency Control", PVLDB 2017) evaluate MVCC specifically. They separate the effects of concurrency-control protocol, version storage, garbage collection and index management, including under contended workloads. Their central observation is that with many threads running in parallel, the synchronisation overhead of MVCC (including shared timestamp and version-chain maintenance) can outweigh the benefits of keeping multiple versions. This matters for a hot row in an MVCC engine: every update to it creates a new version as well as contending on its lock.

Larson et al. ("High-Performance Concurrency Control Mechanisms for Main-Memory Databases", PVLDB 2011, the precursor to Hekaton) report that standard concurrency-control methods do not scale to the transaction rates that main-memory engines can reach. They present optimistic and pessimistic multiversion schemes in which transactions never block during normal processing. These schemes are relevant mainly as MVCC designs whose commit ordering comes from a shared timestamp source.

### Hot tuples held until commit

Guo, Wu, Yan and Yu ("Releasing Locks As Early As You Can", SIGMOD 2021, the Bamboo protocol) target exactly the "every transaction writes one hot row" pattern. They define hotspots as "a small set of tuples frequently read/written by a large number of transactions". They point out that strict two-phase locking makes each transaction hold its hotspot lock until it completes, even when the hotspot access is a small fraction of its run time. As a result, throughput through the hotspot is capped by the whole transaction duration, not by the duration of the hotspot access. Their prototype releases hotspot locks early and tracks dependencies so it can cascade aborts. They evaluate this on synthetic hotspot workloads and TPC-C, showing that serialising only for the hotspot access recovers much of the lost parallelism.

### Commit-time flushes lengthen the serial section

Johnson, Pandis, Stoica, Athanassoulis and Ailamaki (Aether, PVLDB 2010) identify write-ahead logging as a source of serialisation in OLTP workloads made up of small, frequent updates. They name four impediments:

1. I/O-related delays.
2. **Log-induced lock contention**: locks stay held while a transaction waits for its commit record to reach stable storage.
3. Excessive context switching.
4. Contention inside the log buffer.

Their remedies, implemented in Shore-MT, are:

- **Early lock release**, which frees locks once the commit record is in the log buffer, safe because the log flushes in order.
- **Flush pipelining**, a form of group commit that avoids per-transaction context switches.
- **A consolidated log-buffer insert path.**

This is the study most directly about *commit-time* contention. It shows that when a hot lock is held across the commit flush, the serial section on that lock includes the flush latency.

### What carries over to PostgreSQL (inference, not measured by these studies)

Together, the studies support a simple model. Throughput through a single global resource is bounded by one over the time each transaction holds it. Design choices change that hold time a great deal:

- **Atomic, non-transactional allocation** holds the resource only for the instant of the increment. Yu et al. found this to be a real but high ceiling. PostgreSQL sequences take this approach: `nextval` is not rolled back, and its value can be cached per session.
- **A transactional update to one counter or queue row** keeps the row lock until the transaction ends. Under the Bamboo and Aether analyses, every writer then serialises on the remaining transaction time plus the commit flush. In PostgreSQL, a second updater of the same row waits for the first transaction to commit or abort. So with synchronous commit, the hot row's throughput is close to the rate at which commit records can be flushed, unless group commit amortises the flushes.
- **A global lock held through commit** has the same shape as Aether's log-induced lock contention. PostgreSQL's serialisation of committing NOTIFY transactions is an example.

None of the catalogued studies measure PostgreSQL's own row-lock, sequence or NOTIFY paths. Absolute numbers from the DBx1000, Peloton, Shore-MT and Silo prototypes (in-memory engines, sometimes on simulated hardware) should not be read as PostgreSQL figures. Their qualitative ranking of mechanisms is the transferable result.
