# How should a fixed query budget be divided across many event streams or subscriptions with different and time-varying event rates, and does the square-root-of-rate allocation implied by feed-monitoring delay models hold for event-table consumers?

## Question

A consumer, or a group of consumers, has a fixed budget of database queries per
second. It must use that budget to watch many event streams or subscriptions
whose event rates differ and change over time. How should it divide the budget?
And does the square-root-of-rate rule, which comes from delay models for
monitoring RSS feeds, carry over to consumers that read a PostgreSQL event table
by position?

## Findings

### Three scholarly models of "poll many sources on a budget"

Three bodies of work study how to share a limited polling resource among sources
that change at different rates. They use different objectives, and the objective
decides the answer.

1. **Delay (feed monitoring).** Sia, Cho and Cho study how an RSS aggregator
   should monitor many feeds "to retrieve new content quickly using minimal
   resources". Feeds do not push their new postings. Each retrieval fetches
   everything posted since the last one, and the cost to minimise is the delay
   between publication and retrieval. The authors argue that this differs from
   the index-refresh problem a search engine faces. Each posting is a distinct
   item that users want quickly, so every posting adds its own delay. A missed
   posting is not simply replaced by a newer version.
2. **Freshness (web crawling).** Cho and García-Molina study how to keep local
   copies of remote pages "fresh" when "remote data sources … do not notify the
   copies … so we need to periodically poll". Their metric is binary. A copy is
   either current or stale, and the number of changes it missed does not count.
   Under this metric, a uniform refresh rate beats a rate proportional to change
   frequency. The optimal policy gives *less* attention to pages that change too
   often to be kept fresh within the budget. Azar et al. extend the problem by
   weighting pages by request popularity as well as change rate. They show that
   the optimal randomised refresh strategy, which is intractable in general, can
   be computed efficiently to near-optimality.
3. **Waiting cost (polling systems).** Boxma, Levy and Weststrate ask how a
   single server should "efficiently allocate the server's attention among the N
   stations" of a polling table, where each visit to a station costs a switchover
   overhead. Their efficient visit frequencies follow a square-root rule. A
   station's frequency grows with the square root of its waiting-cost weight
   times its arrival rate, adjusted for its load. It falls with the square root
   of its switchover time.

### Why the square-root rule appears, and the assumptions behind it

Suppose each poll of stream *i* costs about *c_i*, whatever it returns. Events
arrive at a long-run rate *λ_i*, polls are evenly spaced at frequency *f_i*, and
the goal is the mean delivery delay per event, weighted by an importance factor
*w_i*. An event waits on average half an interval, 1/(2·f_i). The problem is
then:

- minimise Σ w_i·λ_i / (2·f_i) subject to Σ c_i·f_i = B.

Setting the Lagrangian condition w_i·λ_i / (2·f_i²) = μ·c_i gives:

- **f_i = B · √(w_i·λ_i / c_i) / Σ_j √(c_j·w_j·λ_j)**

When all costs and weights are equal, this reduces to f_i ∝ √λ_i, the
feed-monitoring rule. Adding *c_i* gives the polling-table form, where expensive
visits are made less often. The result does not depend on Poisson arrivals.
For any stationary arrival process whose timing is independent of the poll
phase, the mean wait under periodic polling is half the interval. Randomised
(Poisson) poll times double the mean wait to 1/f_i, because the time to the next
poll is memoryless. Heavy timing jitter therefore costs latency, although the
square-root shape still holds.

How much the rule gains is easy to state. With *N* equal-cost streams, total
rate Λ and *B* polls per second:

- uniform allocation gives a mean event delay of N/(2B);
- rate-proportional allocation also gives exactly N/(2B);
- square-root allocation gives (Σ√λ_i)² / (2·B·Λ), which by Cauchy–Schwarz is
  never worse than N/(2B). The two are equal only when all rates are equal.

For example, take two streams at 99 and 1 events per second with 10 polls per
second. Uniform and proportional allocation both give a mean delay of 100 ms.
Square-root allocation polls about 9.1 times per second and 0.9 times per
second, and cuts the mean delay to about 60 ms. The more skewed the rates, the
bigger the gain. Activity across event streams is usually heavily skewed, so the
gain is usually large.

### Time-varying rates

If a stream's rate *λ(t)* varies predictably, for example on a daily cycle, the
same objective spread over time gives a poll density proportional to
√λ(t). Each poll time *t_j* should satisfy a local optimality condition: the
events expected since the previous poll, ∫ λ from t_{j−1} to t_j, equal
λ(t_j)·(t_{j+1} − t_j). The feed-monitoring model is built for this kind of
periodic posting pattern.

When rates change unpredictably, the allocation is only as good as the rate
estimate. Event-table consumers have a real advantage over crawlers here. A
crawler sees only whether a page changed, so it has to infer change rates from
incomplete histories. An event-table poll returns every new row, so an
exponentially weighted count of rows returned per elapsed second gives a direct,
unbiased rate estimate. Empty-poll backoff can be read as a crude version of
this: a stream with no recent events gets a longer interval.

### Does the rule hold for PostgreSQL event-table consumers?

It holds under specific conditions, and several practical constraints change it.

**Where it holds.** An event-table consumer must deliver every event, so the
delay metric, not freshness, is the right analogue. The freshness optimum, which
starves fast-changing sources, would be *wrong* for an event consumer: it would
let the busiest streams fall furthest behind. The cost structure also fits. A
positional poll (`WHERE stream = $1 AND position > $2 ORDER BY position LIMIT n`
on a suitable index) has a fixed per-query cost: a round trip, planning, and an
index descent. Its per-row cost is incurred once per event however the polls are
scheduled. Only the fixed part depends on the schedule, so it is the budget the
square-root rule divides. This is the same role the switchover time plays in the
polling-table model.

**Where it needs changing.**

- **The objective may be per subscription, not per event.** If each stream or
  subscription has a latency target, or a worst-case bound matters more than the
  mean, every stream's delay is about half its interval whatever its rate. The
  optimum is then uniform polling, or a cap on the longest interval. Under the
  square-root rule, a stream with 1/100 of another's rate waits 10 times longer,
  so quiet streams can breach their targets. A practical compromise is
  constrained water-filling: give every stream at least 1/T_max, then split the
  rest of the budget by the square-root rule.
- **Batch limits add a floor.** If a poll returns at most *L* rows, a stream
  needs f_i ≥ λ_i / L to keep up. A hot stream bound by this floor is no longer
  latency-limited but throughput-limited, and should drain in a loop rather than
  wait for its next slot.
- **Load matters.** In the polling-table model, the load correction reflects
  that a station draining a large backlog per visit needs relatively fewer
  visits. Where handling events takes substantial consumer time, pure
  square-root allocation slightly over-polls the heaviest streams.
- **Shared queries make per-stream allocation unnecessary.** The rule assumes
  each stream has its own query. An event store with a global, monotonically
  increasing position can serve many streams or categories with one query
  ("rows after global position p"). The only remaining decision is that one
  query's interval, set from the budget and the latency target. In polling-table
  terms, merging stations removes the repeated switchover cost. Per-stream
  square-root allocation only pays off when subscriptions really do need
  separate queries, for example filtered category subscriptions on different
  indexes, or independent consumer groups. A hybrid often works best: one shared
  scan for the long tail of quiet streams, and dedicated polls for the few hot
  ones.
- **Notification changes the problem.** With a wake-up signal such as
  LISTEN/NOTIFY, active streams effectively trigger their own polls. The
  remaining polling budget is a safety net against missed signals. Its goal is a
  bounded worst-case delay, which calls for uniform, long fallback intervals,
  not a rate-proportional split.

### Practical answer

When a budget must be split across separately queried subscriptions:

1. allocate f_i ∝ √(w_i·λ̂_i / c_i) using online rate estimates;
2. clamp the result to a per-subscription maximum interval and a
   batch-throughput floor, and re-split the remaining budget;
3. re-solve as rate estimates drift, giving more polls to periods of higher rate
   in proportion to √λ(t).

Where possible, though, avoid per-stream allocation altogether by reading a
shared global position. The square-root rule is a sound default for minimising
mean event delay. It is not the right rule for per-stream latency targets or for
notification fallback polling.
