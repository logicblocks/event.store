# What evaluations measure how randomised jitter in poll timing across many identical consumers changes database load peaks and delivery latency compared with synchronised fixed-interval polling?

## Question

What evaluations measure how randomised jitter in poll timing across many
identical consumers changes database load peaks and delivery latency, compared
with synchronised fixed-interval polling?

## Findings

**No published evaluation in the scholarly record measures this directly.** The
OpenAlex catalogue contains no study that runs many identical consumers polling
a database, with and without randomised jitter, and reports the resulting load
peaks and delivery latency. The relevant evidence comes from three neighbouring
problems that share the same structure: many identical periodic or
event-triggered actors, one shared resource, and randomisation used to spread
their timing. These are periodic routing messages, cache stampedes against
databases, and large populations of periodic wireless devices. Each supplies a
mechanism or a trade-off that carries over. None supplies a measured figure for
PostgreSQL polling.

### Periodic processes synchronise on their own, and abruptly

The foundational study is Floyd and Jacobson's work on the synchronisation of
periodic routing messages. It appeared in ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication
Review and, extended, in IEEE/ACM Transactions on Networking. The authors
examine "many apparently-independent periodic processes" and show one way such
processes "can inadvertently become synchronized". They use simulation and
analysis and give examples of the harm that synchronised updates do to other
traffic. Their central result is that the shift from unsynchronised to
synchronised traffic is "not one of gradual degradation" but "a very abrupt
'phase transition'": adding a single router can tip the system over. The
authors give guidelines for avoiding synchronisation by randomising the
sources. The catalogue's abstract is cut off before it says how much
randomisation they found necessary, so that figure is not reported here.

Three points carry over to event-table consumers:

- **Unaligned start times do not prevent alignment.** Consumers that start at
  different times can still drift into lock-step if anything couples their
  timers. Fixed-interval polling with no random component is the setting the
  study shows to be fragile.
- **Coupling through shared database response times is plausible.** The
  following is an inference that no record measures. A poll loop usually
  sleeps for a fixed interval after its query finishes. Consumers whose
  queries overlap slow each other down, finish together when the contention
  clears, and so start their next intervals together. That is the same kind of
  feedback Floyd and Jacobson studied.
- **Small load tests can mislead.** Because the transition is abrupt, a test
  with a few consumers can show smooth load while one more consumer produces
  synchronised peaks. Evaluations should vary the number of consumers rather
  than test a single count.

### Randomised early action against synchronised load on databases

Vattani, Chierichetti and Lowenstein (Proceedings of the VLDB Endowment)
study the closest database case: the cache stampede. When a popular cache item
expires, many requests miss at the same moment and all regenerate it at once.
The authors say this "severely limits the performance of databases and web
servers". The countermeasure they formalise is to let each process randomly
request regeneration before the expiry time, known as probabilistic early
expiration. They give algorithms they prove optimal and report much better
performance than existing approaches; the abstract is truncated before the
comparison details.

This is the same trade-off as poll jitter. Randomisation turns a synchronised
spike of identical database work into a spread of individual requests. The
price is some work done earlier, or extra work, than a perfectly coordinated
schedule would need. It also shows that the size and placement of the random
component can be optimised rather than picked by rule of thumb.

### Many identical periodic devices on a shared channel

Wireless research reaches the same conclusion with large device populations:

- Kaburaki et al. (IEEE Transactions on Wireless Communications) study
  long-range networks that collect sensor data periodically. They find that a
  simple random-access scheme still suffers "continuous packet collisions"
  under periodic traffic, so sources that collide once tend to keep colliding
  each period. Clock drift in cheap nodes then shifts the alignment over time.
  They propose allocating resources using the periodic pattern and the drift.
  For consumers, a fixed period with no fresh randomness each cycle keeps any
  alignment in place. A single random offset at start-up is weaker than
  drawing new jitter on every poll.
- Laya, Alonso and Alonso-Zárate (IEEE Communications Surveys & Tutorials)
  survey the LTE and LTE-A random-access channel. It is known to "suffer from
  congestion and overloading in the presence of a huge number of devices", and
  the survey reviews alternatives for managing access. Their abstract
  establishes the overload problem but gives no figures for the effect of
  randomisation.

### What the trade-off looks like when stated analytically

The records give no figures for database polling, so the following is standard
renewal-process reasoning rather than measured evidence:

- **Load peaks.** N consumers polling in lock-step every T seconds put N
  queries on the database at the same instant once per period, and contention
  makes each of those queries run longer. If each consumer's phase is
  independently random, the average load is still N/T queries per second. But
  the expected number of concurrent queries falls to about N·d/T, where d is
  the query duration, with only small random variation. The query budget
  barely changes; its shape in time changes a great deal.
- **Delivery latency for a consumer reading its own stream.** Suppose an event
  arrives at a random moment. With a fixed interval T, the mean wait until the
  next poll is T/2 and the worst case is T. Adding zero-mean jitter with
  variance σ² raises the mean wait to T/2 + σ²/(2T); this is the inspection
  paradox. For uniform jitter of ±50% of T, that is about 8% more mean latency,
  and the worst case grows to 1.5T. Jitter that only ever lengthens the sleep
  also raises the mean interval: it cuts query load but adds latency.
- **Delivery latency for competing consumers sharing one stream.** Here,
  spreading the polls directly reduces latency. With N consumers at
  independent random phases, the mean wait until any consumer polls is about
  T/(N+1). In lock-step it stays at T/2, and N−1 of the simultaneous queries
  are redundant or fight over the same rows. Desynchronising therefore
  flattens load peaks and, for shared work, also cuts delivery latency at
  the same query budget.

In short, the literature shows that fixed-interval periodic actors fall into
synchronised behaviour easily and suddenly. It also shows that randomisation
is the established remedy, and that for database stampedes the randomisation
can be tuned optimally. What it does not provide is a measured, side-by-side
comparison of jittered and lock-step polling of a PostgreSQL event table. That
comparison would have to be run as an engineering benchmark that varies the
consumer count, the jitter size and the poll-loop design (fixed-rate versus
sleep-after-completion).
