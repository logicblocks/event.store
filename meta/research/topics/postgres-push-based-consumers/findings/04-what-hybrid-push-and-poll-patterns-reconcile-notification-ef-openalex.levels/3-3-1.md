# How should hysteresis for switching a consumer between notification-driven waiting and continuous batched polling be set so the mode does not oscillate when the arrival rate hovers near the crossover point?

## Question

A consumer can run in two modes. In one it blocks on PostgreSQL `LISTEN` and
reads on each notification. In the other it polls the log continuously in
batches. Each mode is cheaper on one side of a crossover arrival rate. How
should the switch between them be built so the consumer does not flip back and
forth when the arrival rate sits near that crossover?

## Findings

### Short answer

Do not switch on a single arrival-rate threshold. Use two asymmetric
conditions, both driven by the backlog the consumer sees directly in its own
reads:

- **Enter polling fast**, on strong evidence of backlog: a notification-driven
  read returns a full batch, or the smoothed number of events found per
  wake-up passes an upper bound.
- **Leave polling slowly**, only after the log has stayed empty for a dwell
  time `T_off`. Choose `T_off` from the arrival rate at the crossover and the
  highest switch frequency you will accept.

Keep the `LISTEN` subscription live in both modes so that switching costs
almost nothing. The cheaper the switch, the narrower the band can safely be.
No published scheme gives a standard constant for this band. It has to come
from a cost model and measured numbers, as derived below.

### Why a two-threshold band is the right structure

The networking literature on interrupt and polling hybrids states the basic
trade-off directly. Mogul and Ramakrishnan describe interrupt-driven systems
as giving "low overhead and good latency at low offered load", but they
"degrade significantly at higher arrival rates" into receive livelock unless
the system schedules work deliberately. Chang et al. note that the hybrid
interrupt-plus-polling scheme used in Linux and FreeBSD works less well when
"controlling the polling time without regard to the system state". Their
answer is a queue-based adaptive polling scheme. So the switch should be
driven by observed queue state, not by a fixed timer.

Queueing-control theory explains why the policy should have two thresholds and
not one. Heyman studies a single-server M/G/1 queue that pays a start-up cost,
a shut-down cost, a running cost and a holding cost. He proves the optimal
stationary policy is: "turn the server on when n customers are present, and
turn it off when the system is empty". That is a hysteresis band between `n`
and zero, and the paper gives the cost rate exactly as a function of `n`. Lu
and Serfozo generalise the result. In their model, service and arrival rates
are chosen from a finite set whenever the queue length changes, and each
change has a switching cost that depends on both the new and the current
rates. Under submodular cost conditions, the optimal policies are *monotone
hysteretic*: the choice depends on the current mode as well as the queue
length, so the threshold for switching up sits above the threshold for
switching down.

For a consumer, the two "rates" are the two modes. Once switching has any
cost, the best policy is a band. A single threshold is exactly what produces
oscillation near the crossover. Diab studies the same structure for routing
across servers that are kept active or allowed to idle: queueing cost is set
against a switching cost paid on every activation. That work learns the
hysteretic thresholds online when the costs are not known analytically, which
is an option if measured costs drift.

### Mapping the consumer onto the model

The following mapping is reasoning from the models above, not text from the
records.

- **The plain notification-driven consumer already uses a degenerate band.**
  A consumer that wakes on a notification, reads until it gets a partial
  batch, and then waits again is Heyman's policy with `n = 1`: on at the
  first arrival, off when empty. Near the crossover it runs about one on-off
  cycle per arrival burst. Each cycle pays a wake-up plus at least one read
  that returns less than a full batch. That per-burst cycling is the
  oscillation to suppress.
- **The crossover rate is roughly the inverse of the poll interval.** Waiting
  mode costs about `c_wake` per wake-up, and wake-ups track the arrival rate
  `λ`. Polling mode costs about `c_poll` per poll interval `Δ`, whatever the
  load. The crossover is therefore near `λ* ≈ c_poll / (c_wake · Δ)`. When a
  wake-up read and a poll cost about the same, `λ* ≈ 1/Δ`: polling starts to
  win once events arrive more often than the poll interval.
- **The band width follows a square-root rule.** In the standard N-policy
  cost analysis, a fixed switching cost `K` is spread over a cycle containing
  `n` arrivals, and set against a holding (staleness) cost `h` that grows
  linearly with `n`. This gives `n* ≈ sqrt(2·K·λ·(1−ρ)/h)`, where `ρ` is the
  consumer's utilisation. The band only needs to grow with the square root of
  the ratio of switching cost to staleness cost. Making switches cheap
  therefore shrinks the band that is needed.

### Setting the entry condition (waiting to polling)

- **Signal.** Use the number of events each notification-triggered read
  returns, as a fraction of the batch limit `B`.
- **Fast path.** A full batch means the backlog is at least `B` and one read
  cannot keep up. Enter polling immediately on that one observation.
- **Smoothed path.** Otherwise, enter polling when a moving average of events
  per wake-up passes an upper bound `U`. Set `U` to the events per wake-up you
  would expect at `λ_on`, a rate somewhat above `λ*`.
- **Prefer events per read over notification counts.** In PostgreSQL, one
  transaction can append many events, and identical notifications within a
  transaction are collapsed into one. Both distort notification-based rate
  estimates. The literature treats batch arrivals as a separate
  hysteretic-control case: Liu and Tseng study bilevel hysteretic service-rate
  control for a bulk-arrival queue. The catalogue holds no abstract for that
  paper, so only its scope is cited here.

### Setting the exit condition (polling to waiting)

Leave polling only after an idle dwell time `T_off`, never after a single
empty poll. Two calculations give a value for `T_off`. Use whichever is
larger.

1. **Cap the switch rate.** If arrivals are roughly Poisson at rate `λ`,
   gaps longer than `T_off` happen at about `λ·e^(−λ·T_off)` per second. To
   keep switches at the crossover below `r_max`, set
   `T_off = ln(λ*/r_max)/λ*`.
   - Example: `λ* = 20/s` (so `Δ ≈ 50 ms`) and `r_max = 1` per minute give
     `T_off ≈ ln(1200)/20 ≈ 0.36 s`. That is about eight consecutive empty
     polls.
   - Real event streams are burstier than Poisson, with longer idle gaps.
     Where possible, measure the inter-arrival gaps near the crossover and
     set `T_off` to their `1 − r_max/λ*` quantile.
2. **Ski-rental floor.** Choose `M` empty polls so that their cost equals one
   switching cost: `M ≈ K / c_poll`. The standard competitive argument then
   keeps the combined cost of empty polls and switches within about twice
   the best achievable for any arrival pattern. This bound is reasoning, not
   a result from the records.

This "delayed off" pattern matches capacity-management work on unpredictable,
time-varying load. Gandhi et al.'s AutoScale removes idle data-centre servers
only as needed while still meeting response-time targets, and its design is
known for waiting before turning off idle capacity. The catalogue abstract is
cut off before it names the paper's two key features.

### Bounding the band by estimator noise

If the switch uses an explicit rate estimate, the band must be wider than the
estimate's noise.

- An average over a window `w` at rate `λ*` has a standard deviation of about
  `sqrt(λ*/w)` events per second.
- Put `λ_on` and `λ_off` at least `z·sqrt(λ*/w)` above and below `λ*`.
- Example: `z = 3`, `λ* = 20/s` and `w = 5 s` give a margin of ±6/s, so
  `λ_on ≈ 26/s` and `λ_off ≈ 14/s`.
- As a backstop against any remaining chatter, add a minimum time in each
  mode, or a hard limit on switches per minute.

### Making switches cheap

The square-root rule rewards a low switching cost `K`.

- **Never unsubscribe on a mode change.** Keep `LISTEN` active in both modes.
  While polling, drain and discard queued notifications without acting on
  them.
- **The switch back to waiting then needs no extra query.** The last empty
  poll already ran while the subscription was live, so the usual
  subscribe-then-read-then-wait order still holds.
- **Unsubscribing costs more.** A design that calls `UNLISTEN` and `LISTEN` on
  every mode change must repeat the subscribe-then-read sequence on each
  switch back to waiting. That raises `K`, which widens the band needed and
  makes each oscillation more expensive.

### Limits of the evidence

- **The networking schemes are heuristic.** Chang et al. report that adaptive
  hybrid interrupt-polling and interrupt-coalescing schemes "are designed
  using heuristics" and had not been systematically compared, so the field
  offers no standard band constants.
- **The records do not supply the numbers.** Every catalogue abstract was
  truncated, so the specific parameter values in the networking schemes were
  not available.
- **The formulas here are derived.** The crossover estimate, the square-root
  rule, the `T_off` expressions and the noise margin come from standard
  queueing and estimation reasoning built on the cited models. They are not
  quoted from the records, and they should be checked against measured
  per-query costs and arrival traces.

### Recommended configuration

| Setting | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Entry to polling | A full batch on any notification-driven read, or smoothed events per wake-up above `U` |
| Exit to waiting | No events for `T_off`: the larger of `ln(λ*/r_max)/λ*` (or the measured gap quantile) and `K/c_poll` polls |
| Subscription | Never dropped on a mode change |
| Backstop | A minimum time in each mode |
