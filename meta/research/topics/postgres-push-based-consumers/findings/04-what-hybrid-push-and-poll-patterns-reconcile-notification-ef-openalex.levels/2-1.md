# How should a consumer set its safety-net poll interval and adaptive bounds from measured notification loss, acceptable staleness under failure, and the per-poll query cost multiplied across many consumers?

## Question

A consumer treats notifications as wake-up hints over a durable event log and
also runs a safety-net poll. How should it choose that poll's interval, and
the lower and upper bounds of any adaptive range? The inputs are the
notification loss it measures, how much staleness is acceptable when push
fails, and the cost of each poll multiplied across every consumer hitting the
same database.

## Findings

### What the safety-net interval actually controls

The scholarly literature has no paper on PostgreSQL LISTEN/NOTIFY. It does
have mature work on the same trade-off: push-pull data dissemination, cache
leases, failure detectors, crawler refresh scheduling and age of information.
Read together, these works give one framing. When the log is durable and reads
are position-based, a lost notification costs **latency, not correctness**.
The safety-net interval is the knob that turns that latency into a bound.

Gray and Cheriton's lease paper describes the same structure. Clients trust a
time-bounded grant, re-validate when it expires, and as a result "non-Byzantine
failures affect performance, not correctness, with their effect minimized by
short leases." Their analytic model and V-system evaluation found that short
lease terms already give good performance. They also found that the term
matters more as systems grow. A safety-net poll is a lease on the push
channel's trustworthiness: between polls the consumer trusts notifications,
and at each poll it re-checks the log directly.

This framing splits the question into three parts:

1. **Acceptable staleness under failure** sets the *upper* bound.
2. **Fleet-wide poll cost** sets the *lower* bound.
3. **Measured loss** decides where the consumer sits inside that range, and
   when it moves.

Bhide et al.'s adaptive push-pull work uses exactly this shape. Its pull-side
Time-To-Refresh (TTR) adapts to observed change but is always clamped between
a static TTR_min and TTR_max. TTR_min guards against over-polling. TTR_max
makes sure the client keeps checking even when nothing seems to change. Their
combined algorithms, Push-and-Pull and Push-or-Pull, use pull as the fallback
that keeps coherency when push state or the server fails. They argue that push
and pull have complementary strengths in coherency, communication overhead,
state overhead and resilience to failure.

### Upper bound: from acceptable staleness under failure

The worst-case staleness after a silent push failure is roughly the longest
gap between polls, plus the poll's own latency, plus processing time. Chen,
Toueg and Aguilera's failure-detector analysis formalises this. Their detector
sends heartbeats every η and allows a slack δ. Its detection time is bounded
by about η + δ, so the interval and the tolerance together set the guaranteed
worst case.

Translated to a consumer (this is inference, not a claim from the sources):

- Set the *upper end* of the poll interval so that it plus the high-percentile
  poll latency stays within the acceptable failure staleness S_fail.
- If the interval is randomised (see below), the bound applies to the largest
  value the randomiser can produce, not to the nominal period.

Bhide's TTR_max is this value. It is the interval the consumer falls back to
when everything looks healthy, so it should be chosen from the failure budget,
not from typical traffic.

### Lower bound: from per-poll cost across the fleet

**Average load.** With N consumers, each spending database cost c per poll at
interval T, the average load is N·c/T. The smallest interval the fleet can
afford is therefore T_min ≥ N·c / (budgeted share of database capacity). Three
lines of work suggest that budget should be set well below saturation, and
that the *peak* load matters, not just the mean.

- **Queueing reverses the benefit.** Kaul, Yates and Gruteser show that
  "how often should one update" has an interior optimum. Updating too slowly
  leaves information stale. Updating too quickly fills the queue, and the
  waiting time *increases* average age. In their M/M/1 first-come-first-served
  model, age is lowest at roughly half the server's capacity (utilisation
  about 0.53). The number is specific to that model. The lesson carries over:
  past a certain aggregate poll load, shorter intervals make every consumer
  staler, because each poll waits behind the others.
- **Periodic pollers synchronise.** Floyd and Jacobson show that many
  apparently independent periodic processes can lock into step through weak
  coupling. A typical cause is a timer that is reset after handling an event
  that all processes see. The shift from unsynchronised to synchronised is an
  abrupt phase transition, and one extra participant can trigger it. Their
  remedy is to add a large random component to each timer, roughly half the
  period, rather than a small jitter, and to stop timer resets depending on
  shared events.

  For PostgreSQL consumers this is a concrete hazard (inference). A single
  NOTIFY wakes every listener at once. If each consumer resets its safety-net
  timer whenever it does a notification-driven read, the whole fleet's
  safety-net polls line up, and N·c/T arrives as bursts of N·c. The same
  happens when all consumers reconnect together after a database restart.
  Draw the interval from a wide range, for example about [0.5T, 1.5T], and
  size T_max against the top of that range.
- **Fixed budgets favour uniform allocation.** Cho and Garcia-Molina study
  how to spend a fixed polling budget across sources that change at different
  rates. Spreading the budget evenly beats giving each source a share
  proportional to its change rate, and the optimal policy may poll the
  fastest-changing sources *less*, because extra polls of a fast-changing
  source add little freshness. For a consumer fleet sharing one database, the
  per-consumer safety-net rate should therefore not simply grow with stream
  activity.

If T_min comes out larger than T_max, polling alone cannot meet the staleness
target at that fleet size. The options are then to make each poll cheaper, to
reduce how many consumers poll independently, or to detect channel failure
another way so that the steady-state interval can be relaxed.

### Using measured notification loss

**What to measure (inference).** The most useful loss signal is the fraction
of events, or of read batches, that a consumer first sees through a
safety-net poll rather than through a notification-triggered read. Only those
discoveries show that the push channel missed something.

Chen et al. show how to turn such measurements into settings. Their procedure
takes the measured message-loss probability and the mean and variance of
message delay. Against bounds on detection time and false-detection rate, it
returns the *largest* sending interval that meets those bounds, which is the
cheapest one. Applied here, the consumer would choose the longest safety-net
interval that keeps both worst-case and expected loss-induced staleness within
target, given the loss rate it has observed.

**Isolated versus correlated loss (an illustrative model, not from the
sources).** The *structure* of the loss matters more than its rate.

- With position-based reads, an isolated lost notification is repaired by the
  next delivered notification for that consumer, because the next read picks
  up everything after the stored position. Assume Poisson arrivals at rate λ
  and independent loss probability p. The expected staleness of a lost event
  is then about (1 − e^(−λ(1−p)T)) / (λ(1−p)). For busy streams this is
  roughly the time between events, and the safety net hardly matters. For
  quiet streams it approaches T.
- Correlated loss behaves differently. Examples are a dropped listener
  connection, the window before LISTEN takes effect after a reconnect, or a
  consumer that is not listening at all. In these cases *every* notification
  is lost until recovery, and the safety-net poll is the only thing that
  helps.

So the upper bound has to be sized for the correlated-outage case. The
measured isolated-loss rate mostly decides how often polls are wasted.

### Adaptive bounds: what to adapt on

Classic adaptive pull shortens the interval when the source changes often.
Bhide et al.'s TTR follows the observed rate of change and recent coherency
violations. Bright, Gal and Raschid's history-based policies estimate when
updates will happen, with extra handling for update bursts and for sources
with too little history, to reduce probes while meeting freshness targets.
Bozdag and van Deursen likewise argue for switching between push and pull at
run time instead of fixing one approach.

For a *safety-net* poll the right trigger is different (inference). The
interval should react to evidence that push failed, not to the change rate,
because changes that arrive with notifications are already handled. A
reasonable policy within [T_min, T_max] is:

1. **Steady state.** Run near T_max, with wide random jitter, on a timer that
   is not reset by notification-driven reads, or is reset only with fresh
   jitter.
2. **Suspected outage.** When the listener reconnects, LISTEN is
   re-established, or a poll finds events that were never announced, poll
   once immediately to catch up. Then drop to T_min, matching Bhide's fallback
   to pull when push fails.
3. **Recovery.** After k consecutive polls find nothing unannounced, increase
   the interval multiplicatively back towards T_max.
4. **Overload.** If poll latency rises, which signals the queueing effect
   Kaul et al. describe, raise T_min rather than poll harder.

If channel liveness can be checked directly, for example with periodic
heartbeat notifications, Chen et al.'s detection-time and accuracy analysis
applies to that check. The safety-net poll then only needs to cover isolated
loss, so its upper bound can be relaxed.
