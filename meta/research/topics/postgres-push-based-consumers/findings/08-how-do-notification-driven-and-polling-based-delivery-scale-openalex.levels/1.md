# How do notification-driven and polling-based delivery scale as listeners, channels, subscriptions and event throughput grow — measured notification latency and throughput, commit-time contention, thundering-herd wake-ups and fan-out cost — and what does published research on push versus pull delivery and adaptive polling say about the latency-versus-load trade-off?

## Question

How do notification-driven (push) and polling-based (pull) delivery behave as the number of listeners, channels, subscriptions and the event rate grow? The question covers notification latency and throughput, contention at commit time, thundering-herd wake-ups and the cost of fan-out. It also asks what published research on push versus pull and on adaptive polling says about trading latency against load. The context is replacing fixed-interval polling consumers in a PostgreSQL-only event store with notification-based delivery.

## Findings

### What the scholarly record does and does not cover

The peer-reviewed literature indexed in OpenAlex contains no measurements of PostgreSQL `LISTEN` and `NOTIFY` itself. No record reports notification latency, notification throughput or commit-time contention as a function of how many listeners, channels or notifying writers there are. The quantitative, PostgreSQL-specific parts of the question therefore remain open and need engineering sources: the server's implementation, its mailing-list history and practitioner benchmarks.

What the literature does provide is a consistent body of work on the general trade-off: push versus pull dissemination, interrupts versus polling under load, waking every waiter versus waking one, and polling within a resource budget. Its conclusions apply closely, though only by analogy, to the choice between notifying and polling consumers. Where this note relates that work to PostgreSQL, it relies on the database's documented behaviour: a notification is delivered, at commit, to every session listening on its channel. That mapping is inference and does not come from the cited records.

### Push and pull have complementary costs, and adaptive hybrids beat both

Bhide et al. (IEEE Transactions on Computers, 2002) set out the two basic techniques for keeping clients current with changing data. In the pull model, clients must "frequently pull the data based on the dynamics of the data and a user's coherency requirements". In the push model, servers "maintain state information pertaining to clients and push only those changes that are of interest to a user". The authors say the two "have complementary properties", beginning with the level of temporal coherency each maintains (the catalogue excerpt is cut off at that point). Their answer, in both the journal paper and its earlier conference version (Deolasee et al.), is an adaptive combination of push and pull rather than either one alone.

Two broadcast studies say the same. Acharya, Franklin and Zdonik (SIGMOD 1997) take a push-only broadcast design aimed at "very large client populations" and examine how to balance it with client-initiated pull. Hu and Chen (IEEE Transactions on Vehicular Technology) observe that broadcast traffic "changes dynamically" and propose a mechanism with "dynamic traffic awareness" that adjusts the push–pull time balance as load changes. The common result is that no fixed mode is best at every load. The best balance moves with client population and update rate, so the delivery mechanism should adapt.

Applied to PostgreSQL (inference): `NOTIFY` is the push side. The server tracks which sessions listen on which channels and sends every notification to all of them, so its cost grows with listeners and events. A polling consumer is the pull side. Its cost grows with the number of consumers and the poll frequency, regardless of how many events arrive.

### Per-event notification wins at low load and degrades at high load

Mogul and Ramakrishnan (ACM Transactions on Computer Systems, 1997) give the standard account of the latency-versus-load trade-off for per-event wake-ups. Interrupt-driven systems "can provide low overhead and good latency at low offered load, but degrade significantly at higher arrival rates". At the extreme comes receive livelock, where "the system spends all of its time processing interrupts", and "no packets are delivered to the user application". The catalogue excerpt is cut off at their remedy. The paper is widely known for scheduling notification handling deliberately: it polls under heavy load and keeps interrupts under light load.

The PostgreSQL analogue (inference) is one notification per committed append, one wake-up per listening consumer per notification, and one read query per wake-up. Total cost then scales with event rate times listener count, and at high rates the per-notification work pushes out useful work. A poller's cost stays flat, and each poll returns a larger batch as the event rate rises. This supports a hybrid design:

- Treat a notification as a hint that work exists, not as the work itself.
- Merge bursts of hints into one wake-up.
- After waking, read in batches until the consumer has caught up.
- Only then go back to waiting for notifications.

### Waking every listener, and thundering herds

The operating-systems literature puts a number on the cost of waking every waiter. The Linux Scalability Project (Honeyman et al.) found a thundering herd in `accept`, where all waiting threads were woken for each connection. Changing it to "awaken only one, instead of all, waiting threads" improved macro-benchmark performance "by over 50% on high-performance hardware".

Pan et al. (Hermes, 2025) show that waking only one waiter is not a full fix. Early `epoll` "suffered from the thundering herd problem". Exclusive wake-ups (Linux 4.5) avoid it but cause LIFO wake-ups that concentrate work "on a few workers". Port-reuse hashing spreads the work but "suffers from hash collisions and lacks awareness of worker load". The lesson is that removing wasted wake-ups needs load-aware dispatch, not just a single wake-up.

Sivaraman and Liu describe a related failure in DNS. Caching resolvers see "a regular pattern of spikes in queries", that is, synchronised periodic load, and the authors recommend software mitigations.

Applied to PostgreSQL (inference), notifications are wake-all by design. This matters in two cases:

- **Competing consumers.** When several consumers share one work stream on one channel, every event wakes all N of them, and at most one gets the work. The other N−1 wake up, run a query and find nothing, so wasted work per event grows linearly with N.
- **Independent subscriptions.** When every subscriber needs every event, the fan-out is real work rather than waste, but its cost is still events times listeners.

Pollers on a fixed interval that start together, or that all react to the same notification, can fall into step and produce the periodic spikes the DNS work describes. Adding jitter to intervals breaks that synchrony.

### Subscription matching and channel granularity

Carzaniga, Rosenblum and Wolf (ACM Transactions on Computer Systems, 2001) name the two main services of an event-notification infrastructure. The first is "notification selection", meaning "determining which notifications match which" subscriptions; the second is delivering them. Eugster et al. (ACM Computing Surveys, 2003) describe subscribers that "register their interest in an event, or a pattern of events" and are then "asynchronously notified". They also survey the many variants that differ in how expressive that interest can be.

PostgreSQL notifications offer only selection by channel name, which is the simplest topic-based form (inference). Any filtering finer than one channel must therefore happen in one of two places:

- **More channels.** This means more registrations per session and more state for the server to match against.
- **The consumer.** Consumers receive notifications they then ignore, which are wasted wake-ups.

Channel granularity is therefore the main control over fan-out cost. One global channel minimises server-side state but maximises wasted wake-ups. One channel per stream or category reduces wasted wake-ups at the cost of more registrations.

### Polling within a budget

Cho and García-Molina (ACM Transactions on Database Systems, 2003) study the pure-pull case, where sources "do not notify the copies… of new changes, so we need to periodically poll the sources". Because "polling the sources takes significant time and resources, it is very difficult to keep the copies completely up-to-date". The design problem becomes how to spend a limited polling budget.

Simple analysis (inference, not measured in the sources) shows where fixed-interval polling sits on the latency-versus-load curve. With interval T and events arriving uniformly, the average delivery delay is about T/2 and the worst case is about T. Database load is roughly N consumers divided by T queries per second, regardless of the event rate. That load is wasted when the system is idle and is efficient when it is busy, because each poll returns a batch. Adaptive polling (backing off after empty polls and polling continuously while results keep arriving) moves along this curve. Without some form of notification, though, it cannot get below the T/2 latency floor at the idle end.

### Overall answer

Taken together, the research points to the same latency-versus-load trade-off. Push gives the lowest latency and no idle cost at low event rates. Its cost grows with events times listeners, it wastes wake-ups when it wakes every listener, and at high rates its per-event overhead dominates. Pull has bounded, predictable load and batches naturally under heavy load, but its latency is tied to the poll interval and it costs the most when the system is idle.

Every source that proposes a remedy prefers an adaptive hybrid to either pure form: Bhide et al., Acharya et al., Hu and Chen, and Mogul and Ramakrishnan. For a PostgreSQL consumer that means the following (design inference):

- Use notifications as merged wake-up hints.
- Read in batches until caught up.
- Keep a long, jittered fallback poll to catch notifications missed during reconnects.
- Avoid broadcasting wake-ups to competing consumers, either by partitioning channels or by having one listening session hand work to a pool.

The actual PostgreSQL numbers are not in the scholarly record. These include notification latency, how much a heavily notifying workload reduces commit throughput, and how the server's notification queue behaves as listeners grow.
